## 1. Hook

[warm, unhurried, like you're telling a friend]

Picture a hillside at dusk. A man stands at the gate of a stone sheep pen, counting as the flock goes in. Ninety-seven. Ninety-eight. Ninety-nine.

[beat]

He counts again. Ninety-nine.

One is missing.

Most of us would call that a pretty good day. Ninety-nine percent. Cut your losses and get some sleep. But Jesus once told a story about a shepherd who doesn't do the math that way. He leaves the ninety-nine and walks out into the dark for the one.

And Jesus told that story to explain why he spent his time with the very people everyone else had written off.

So who is the one? And what does this story tell us about a God who goes looking?

Let's walk through it.

## 2. The scene

[settle in, conversational]

Luke chapter fifteen doesn't open with a story. It opens with a scene.

[read slowly]

[NLT Luke 15:1-2: Dominique pastes exact text]

[pause]

Tax collectors in Jesus' day weren't just unpopular accountants. They collected taxes for Rome, the occupying empire, and many took extra for themselves. To their neighbors, they were collaborators and cheats. And "sinners" was a catch-all for people known to live outside God's law, the people respectable folks kept at arm's length.

These are the people crowding in to hear Jesus.

And standing at the edge of the crowd are the Pharisees and the teachers of religious law. These were serious, devout men. Many of them genuinely wanted Israel to be faithful to God. But they're watching Jesus, and they're muttering.

The problem isn't only that he talks to these people. It's that he eats with them. In that culture, sharing a meal meant acceptance, even friendship. A teacher at the table with tax collectors looked like a teacher saying, "God's fine with these people as they are."

So the grumbling is really a question. Why would a holy man welcome people like that?

Jesus answers it, not with an argument, but with three stories. The first one is about a sheep.

## 4. What it meant then

[teaching voice, clear]

So what would Jesus' first listeners have heard in this story? Let's take it in three parts.

[Part one]

Part one: the shepherd.

To them, a shepherd wasn't a soft figure on a greeting card. Shepherding was hard, outdoor work. Long days, cold nights, wild animals, rough country. And a sheep alone in the wilderness is in real danger.

But the shepherd was also one of the oldest pictures in Israel's scriptures for God himself. "The Lord is my shepherd," David wrote. And the prophets used the same picture for Israel's leaders, who were supposed to shepherd God's people.

In Ezekiel thirty-four, God confronts those leaders for neglecting the flock. They didn't care for the weak. They didn't bring back the ones who strayed. They didn't go looking for the lost. And then God makes a promise.

[read]

[NLT Ezekiel 34:11: Dominique pastes exact text]

Now picture the Pharisees hearing Jesus tell this story. They were leaders. They were meant to be shepherds. And here is Jesus, out among the strays, doing exactly what God said he himself would do.

[Part two]

Part two: the ninety-nine and the one.

Some people hear this and think, who leaves ninety-nine sheep to go find one? Jesus doesn't tell us who's watching the ninety-nine, because that isn't his point. His point is the value of the one. To this shepherd, the lost sheep isn't one percent of an asset. It's his, and he won't settle for ninety-nine.

And then there's that last line, about the ninety-nine righteous people who don't need to repent. You can almost hear a gentle edge in Jesus' voice. The people grumbling saw themselves as the safe ninety-nine. Jesus doesn't argue. He lets the question hang in the air. Is anyone really in that group? The rest of Luke's Gospel suggests the answer is no. Everyone needs to be found. The real difference is whether we know it.

[Part three]

Part three: joy in heaven.

Listen for the word that keeps coming back. Joy. The shepherd is joyful. His neighbors rejoice. And heaven itself rejoices.

That's the heart of his answer. The Pharisees looked at Jesus eating with sinners and saw a scandal. Jesus says, you're looking at a celebration. Every one of those meals was a small picture of heaven's party. The question for the grumblers wasn't whether those people deserved a seat. It was whether they'd come in and celebrate too.

## 5. Three lost things

[lighter, connecting the dots]

The lost sheep is only the first of three stories Jesus tells in a row, and they're meant to be heard together.

Next, a woman with ten silver coins loses one. She lights a lamp, sweeps the whole house, and hunts high and low until it turns up. Then she calls her friends and neighbors to celebrate, and Jesus says the angels of God share that joy whenever one sinner turns back.

Then comes the third story, the one we looked at in our first episode: the Prodigal Son. A younger son takes his inheritance, wastes it far from home, and finally comes to his senses. And while he's still far down the road, his father sees him, and runs.

Look at the pattern. One sheep out of a hundred. One coin out of ten. One son out of two. Each time the loss gets more personal, what was lost is found, and it ends in a party.

But the third story has one more scene. The older brother stands outside, angry, refusing to go in to the celebration. Sound familiar? He's standing exactly where the grumbling Pharisees stood in verse two. The whole chapter is one answer to one complaint, and it ends with the father pleading with his older son to come inside.

One more thing. The sheep and the coin can't do anything to get found. The son does turn around and head home. Put the three stories side by side and you see both halves of the gospel. God searches for us long before we ever look for him. And he invites us to turn and come home.

If you haven't watched our Prodigal Son episode yet, I'll link it at the end of this video and in the description.

## 6. What it means for you

[slower, personal, straight to camera in your mind]

So what does this mean for you?

Maybe you know exactly what it's like to be the one. Not through some dramatic rebellion, necessarily. Sheep don't usually run away. They wander. One patch of grass, then the next, then the next, until they look up and nothing looks familiar. Maybe that's how faith slipped for you. Not a decision. Just a drift.

If that's you, hear what this story says about God. He isn't waiting at the gate with his arms crossed, keeping score. He's already out looking. He searches until he finds. And when he finds you, he doesn't drive you home. He carries you. And he's glad.

Jesus said it plainly about himself, as a guest in the home of another tax collector, a man named Zacchaeus.

[read]

[NLT Luke 19:10: Dominique pastes exact text]

In John's Gospel, Jesus calls himself the good shepherd, the one who lays down his life for the sheep. The shoulders that carry the lost sheep home are the same shoulders that carried a cross.

[beat]

Or maybe you're more like the ninety-nine. You've been around faith a long time. If so, this story has a question for you too. When someone far from God walks through the door, do you grumble, or do you celebrate? Heaven celebrates. And we get to join in.
